Abstract— Plant diseases are a major challenge in producing 
agriculture products, especially in areas where the diagnosis is 
often made through simple visual inspection and traditionally 
poor access to experts. While deep learning methods have 
proven to be very accurate in a controlled setting, real-world 
implementation is limited because of high computational 
demands and difficulty in clear decisions and usability for 
farmers. To circumvent these problems, in this paper, we 
propose a lightweight and interpretable plant disease detection 
model named XAI-Plant, that is designed to work in real-time 
on resource constrained device. The proposed system employs a 
small convolutional neural network optimized for execution on 
a CPU combined with a version of Grad-CAM explainability 
module that highlights the disease affected areas in the leaf 
images. In addition, a multifaceted (multilingual) output 
interface is incorporated in order to enhance accessibility of 
users with different linguistic backgrounds. The model is then 
trained with a combination of benchmark data sets and images 
collected in the field in order to assure robustness during actual 
farming conditions. Experimental evaluation demonstrate that 
the proposed system has 95.1% accuracy of controlled data set 
and 92.3% accuracy of field data while also keeping low 
calculation. The results present that useful plant disease 
diagnosis can be achieved with interpretable and efficient 
artificial intelligence models, giving rise to feasible support for 
agricultural applications.

I. INTRODUCTION  
Agriculture plays an important role in promoting food 
security and livelihoods and often times crop productivity is 
impacted by plant diseases which are hard to identify at the 
early stage. In many farming regions, diagnosing disease still 
relies on subjective visual examination by farmers, which is 
usually not accurate. Such traditional practices can lead to 
delayed intervention and improper treatment as well as heavy 
yield loss particularly where the access to agricultural experts 
and the diagnostic facilities are limited.  
Lately there are advances in deep learning that allow us to do 
the same automated medical diagnosis for plant disease with 
a lot of accuracy on the benchmark plant datasets. 
Convolutional neural networks-based methods have been 
shown to achieve good performance when used to detect 
disease patterns in leaf images in controlled laboratory setups. 
However, their effectiveness is often reduced when applied 
in the actual agricultural setting, such as under a difference in 
lighting, background clutter, leaf orientation and image 
quality. In addition, many existing models of deep learning 
demand a lot of computational resources, limiting their use 
on low cost mobile or edge devices commonly used by 
farmers.  
Another critical limitation of current plant disease detection 
system is the lack of explainability. Most of the deep learning 
algorithms are usually black boxes where you get the answer 
without any obvious visual or logical justification. This leads 
to loss of user trust and makes it hard for farmers to make any 
sense of or validate the diagnosis. Furthermore, many of the 
existing solutions offer interfaces in only one language, 
limiting the usability of the systems in agricultural 
communities with a linguistically diverse population of 
farmers where farmers prefer to use local or regional 
languages.  
To overcome these problems, in this paper, XAI-Plant, a 
lightweight, interpretable plant disease detection model for 
real-time scheme implementation in real-world farming 
context, is proposed. The proposed system balances the 
tradeoff between classification accuracy and computer 
efficiency, combines explainable AI techniques to allow 
visual insights into model predictions, and has multilingual 
output to improve accessibility. By working together, 
lightweight model design, explainability and evaluation using 
both benchmark datasets and field-collected datasets is aimed 
at the unmet need of bridging the gap between experimental 
AI research and deployable agricultural solutions. 
II. LITERATURE SURVEY 
Early methods to detect plant diseases were based on hand-
crafted features of images such as color statistics, texture 
descriptor and simple statistical measures to identify the 
difference between healthy and infected leaves. As was 
computationally efficient, these methods were very sensitive 


==================== PAGE 2 ====================

to changes in lighting conditions and background complexity, 
thus reducing their usefulness in field environments [1]. 
With the introduction of deep learning, convolutional neural 
network architectures [2], [3] such as ResNet, Inception, and 
EfficientNet were used to enhance significantly classification 
accuracy on standard benchmark plant disease data sets [2], 
[3]. Despite their effectiveness, these high-capacity models 
are computationally expensive and demand high memory 
usage, hence, are not contributable for specifying it on low 
power devices. Looking at the other side, their performance 
sometimes tends to drop when tested in the real world on 
images affected with natural noise, shadows and irregular leaf 
orientations [4], [5]. 
To cope with the computational limitations, MobileNetV2, 
ShuffleNet, 
EfficientNet-Lite 
are 
some 
lightweight 
architectures that aim to decrease the complexity of the 
models without losing the acceptable accuracy [4], [6]. 
Additional 
optimization 
techniques, 
like 
pruning, 
quantization and knowledge distillation have further 
increased the runtime efficiency [7]. However, aggressive 
compression can have a negative impact on the prediction 
stability, especially in uncontrolled conditions in the field [6]. 
In parallel, the area of explainable artificial intelligence has 
seen the emergence of visualization techniques, such as Grad-
CAM, Score-CAM and LIME, to highlight regions of images 
that explains model predictions [8], [9]. Concept-based 
methods such as ACE further attempt to expose higher-level 
patterns learned by the model [10]. Although these 
techniques enhance transparency, most remain experimental 
and are rarely integrated into deployable plant disease 
diagnosis systems. 
Recent studies have demonstrated the feasibility of edge-
based inference using compact models, yet challenges related 
to multilingual accessibility, interpretability, and robustness 
across diverse environments remain unresolved [11], [14], 
[15]. Consequently, there is a lack of unified frameworks that 
combine 
computational 
efficiency, 
real-time 
edge 
deployment, explainability, and multilingual user support. 
This gap motivates the development of the proposed XAI-
Plant framework. 
III. DATA COLLECTION AND PREPROCESSING 
The quality and diversity of images used during the training 
of any model used in plant disease detection is very useful in 
how well the model will perform. Since most of diseases 
manifest themselves through visible changes, such as 
discoloration, lesions, fungal patches or texture distortions, it 
is important that the dataset covers these variations at 
different environmental conditions. In this section, the dataset 
applied to the XAI-Plant framework and the preprocessing 
steps that were followed to prepare these images before 
sending them to the network are explained. 
A. Dataset Description 
In order to ensure that the system will operate reliably for real 
farming conditions, two kinds of datasets were used, 
including a structured benchmark dataset and a set of directly 
taken images from fields. This approach helps the model to 
learn clear features of diseases, and also adapt to the 
unpredictable nature of outdoor environments. 
1. Plant Village Dataset 
The Plant Village dataset has more than 54,000 labeled 
images in total from 14 crop species and more than 35 
diseases which include healthy samples. These images were 
made in controlled conditions where lighting and plain 
backgrounds were used. As a result, they clearly suggest the 
symptoms of the disease such as: 
 
fungal or mold-like growth, 
 
streaks and blotches that are indicators of viral 
infections, 
 
bacterial spots, 
 
early signs of nutrient of nutrient related discoloration. 
Since we have a clean and uniform dataset, it helps us to form 
a strong base with the learning of the basic visual key features 
of each disease. 
2.Field-Collected Images 
In order to prove the practicality of the system, 3,250 images 
of leaves were taken from farms in the region with regular 
smartphone cameras. These images are representative of the 
wide variety of challenges seen in natural environments 
including: 
 
Irregular lighting - harsh sunlight, heavy shadows, 
glare; 
 
Background interference - soil, other plants, stems, 
insects; 
 
Partial visibility - Folded leaves, over-lapping leaves, 
damaged areas; 
 
Resolution variations - ranging from low resolution to 
full HD; 
 
Natural artifacts - particles of dust, droplets of water, 
insect bites. 
Including these samples helps the model understand how 
diseases would look outside the lab setting so that the model 
would achieve more reliable results when the farmer takes a 
picture in the field. 
B. Image Preprocessing 
Since images are taken from different sources and appears 
different, the preprocessing is essential in order to make them 
consistent and useful 
for training. The XAI-plant 
preprocessing pipeline has several steps to ensure that the 
images are as standard as possible and to emphasize the 
specifics of each disease. 
 Resizing-The size of every single image is resized to 224 x 
224 pixels. This size is consistent with the size of the 
expected input to the lightweight CNN, and is necessary to 
ensure that all images have the same size when being trained 
or tested. 
Normalization-To 
mitigate 
this 
effect 
of 
lighting 
differences, the value of pixels are scaled to a 0-1 range. This 
step helps the model to learn more efficiently by making sure 
that images that were taken in sunlight or shade do not 
introduce any unnecessary variations. 
Color Space Standardization-All images are preserved as 
RGB images. Since many diseases manifest themselves by 
subtle color variations such as yellowing, browning or 
darkening at the border of lesions it is mandatory to preserve 
the original color information in order to classify diseases 
correctly. 
Noise Reduction-Field images are much more likely to have 
blur, uneven illumination or even visual noise as a result of 


==================== PAGE 3 ====================

wind or camera movement. Severely damaged images are 
removed during the cleaning of data sets.  
Data Augmentation-Because some categories of diseases 
contain fewer samples than others, augmentation techniques 
are employed to increase the number of examples in the 
dataset that the model is learning from, to enhance the 
model's ability to generalize. The following transformations 
are followed: 
 
Rotations (+/- 20deg) in order to simulate the different 
view angles, 
 
Horizontal and vertical flips to make various mirrored 
ones, 
 
Zoom manages to mimic close and far captures, 
 
Brightness is varied in order to imitate field lighting 
variations, 
 
Light blurring in order to replicate minor camera shake. 
Such adjustments produce more examples and non-
overfitting to clean laboratory images.

C. Train- Validation- Test Split 
To test the system fairly, the data set combined is split into 
the: 
 
80% for training, 
 
20% for validation, 
 
A completely different and independent test set 
comprised entirely of field-captured images. 
When using field images for the final test, the accuracy 
indicated in the results reflects performing on the field, not 
performing on controlled sets of images. 
D. Importance of Preprocessing 
The above described preprocessing steps are a key part in 
improving the reliability of the XAI-plant framework. By 
standardizing image sizes, adding noise reduction, preserving 
color patterns and introducing augmented variations, the 
model is more prepared to spot characteristics of the diseases 
in a consistent way regardless of numerous varied real-world 
conditions. These measures help the system to maintain its 
strong performance, also in the face of being taken by farmers 
with basic mobile devices in unpredictable environments. 
IV. PROPOSED METHODOLOGY 
The principles guiding the development of the XAI-Plant 
system were that it should be accurate, interpretable and of 
practical use in agricultural environments. This section 
presents those guiding principles and describes the methods 
used for using the iPSys computational approaches and 
architectural workflow for enabling the plant disease 
detection to be real-time and based on resource-limited 
devices. 
A. Design Principles 
1. Light Weight and Efficient Operation 
Most of the farmers are using cells phones/low power 
computer makes it essential that the model can perform 
efficiently without specialty hardware. XAI-Plant is based on 
a small convolutional neural network (CNN) that can be fully 
operated on CPU-based systems. The resulting final model is 
of the order of 14.2 MB, which makes it possible to infer very 
quickly, even in an offline environment, without requiring a 
cloud environment to be processed. 
2.Explainability is Integrated and Users Trust 
Automated predictions for users mean nothing unless they 
have access to information about the basis of the decision. To 
support this, explanation is baked directly into the system 
using Grad-CAM. Every prediction is accompanied by a 
heatmap which shows areas of the leaf of greatest importance 
in relation to the classification. This transparency is used to 
assist farmers in validating the diagnosis and recognizing 
characteristic symptoms themselves. 
3.Multilingual Accessibility for Farmers 
Agricultural users frequently prefer to deal in their regional 
languages. To make the system widely usable, XAI-plant 
returns results in English, Hindi and Marathi with flexible 
design so that other languages can be incorporated. This is for 
the sake of making it understandable for other people that 
may not be familiar with technical terms. 
4.Scalability and Flexibility of deployment 
XAI-plant is designed as a modular system, that is able to 
adapt to different deployment environment. It can run as a 
mobile entirely off-the-network tool, or as an embedded 
conjunction into local server and cloud-effect based 
environments for larger distribution. Parts like preprocessing, 
inference, explainability, knowledge retrieval can work in 
isolation and in combination depending on the user's needs. 
5.Action-Oriented Output 
Beyond identifying diseases, the system makes suggestions 
about treating the diseases and steps to prevent them. This 
ensures that the outcome of the diagnosis is an immediate, 
practical step, without the user needing to look for further and 
external guidance. 
B. System Architecture  
The workflow of the XAI-Plant is composed of five modules 
one after the other: 
 
Image Input Layer-Accept leaf images captured from 
leaf declaring devices or posted by using a web 
interface. 
 
Preprocessing Layer-Does standardised operations 
such as resize, normalise, noise handling, etc. Also does 
augmentation during training. 
 
Light weight CNN Feature Extractor-A custom CNN 
is made to process the image and learn the 
characteristics of the disease relating to color, texture, 
and structural features of the disease.

 
Module for explaining the decisions (Grad-CAM)-
Produces class activation maps for visualising parts of 
the image that affect the prediction. 
 
Multilingual Output Module-Generates the diagnosis, 
confidence score, visualization of the heatmap and 
relevant treatment advice in the language chosen by the 
user.

C. Lightweight CNN Design of Model 
The CNN model taking part in XAI-Plant is a mix of accuracy 
and efficiency, which can be exercised on devices because 
they have limited memory and processing power. 
Layer Structure 
 
Conv Block 1 32 filters (3*3) ReLU-> MaxPooling 
 
Conv Block 2 64 Filters (3*3) ReLU Maxpooling 
 
Conv Block 3: 128 filters (3×3), ReLU 
 
Global Average Pooling: Reduces feature maps into a 
small sized feature vector 
 
Dense Layer 128 units ReLU Dropout 0.4 
 
Output Layer Softmax classifier Multiclass prediction 
Model Properties 
 
Private Parameters: trainable parameters: 1.46 million 
 
Model size: ~14.2 MB 
 
Maximum memory consumption: ~38MB when doing 
inference 
This architecture has the benefit of allowing reliable 
performance in the field with no need for high-performance 
hardware. 
D. Workflow of Explainability Integration (XAI Workflow) 
XAI-plant has explainability as an integral part instead of an 
add-on. The following is the workflow in operation of the 
classifier: 
 
The last conv layer provides spatial activations maps. 
 
The gradients of a predicted class with respect to these 
maps are calculated. 
 
Weighted activations are combined to provide a class-
specific mask of activations. 
 
The mask is oversized to make it similar to the original 
image and superimposed to generate a Grad-CAM 
heatmap. 
The visualization that results is used by users to understand 
the prediction and gain confidence in decisions made by the 
system.

VI. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE 
This paper introduced XAI-Plant, which is a wire-light and 
interpretable deep learning method for detecting plant 
diseases that is intended for real-world agriculture 
applications. The suggested system incorporates CNN with 
Grad-CAM based visual explanations and multilingual 
output interface, which facilitates effective operation on 
resource constrained devices such as smartphones and edge 
systems. Experimental evaluation with both benchmark and 
field-collected datasets showed uniform performance (92%-
95%) with low model size that can be used for local inference. 
Although the system operates under various conditions 
reliably, some limitations always remain. Diseases with 
similar symptoms with one another, especially in early stages, 
can 
cause 
decreased 
confidence 
in 
classification. 
Additionally, kinds of diseases with few training examples 
have lower recall. The explainability module though 
succeeds to show affected regions may not convey fine-
grained variation of symptoms in complex cases. The Future 
work will concentrate on increasing the diversity of the field 
collected datasets to include seasonal variations and early-
stage disease samples. The optimization to perform in 
hardware accelerators like mobile CPUs and edge AI devices 
will also be explored. Enhancements to interpretability 
section techniques in addition to the integration of contextual 
environmental data might aid additional improve in 
alternative diagnostic reliability for sensible farm settings.
